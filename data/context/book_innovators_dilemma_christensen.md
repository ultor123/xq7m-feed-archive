# The Innovator's Dilemma — Clayton Christensen
> Distilled for speaking, pitching, and daily insight. Framework notes in own words.

## Core Thesis
Well-run companies fail *because* they are well-run. Listening to your best customers, protecting fat margins, and investing where the biggest current market is — all textbook good management — are exactly what blinds incumbents to disruption. The dilemma: the very practices that make you great at the current game make you lose the next one.

## Sustaining vs Disruptive Innovation
- **Sustaining innovation** — makes an existing product *better* on the dimensions mainstream customers already value. Incumbents almost always win these; they have the resources and the motivation.
- **Disruptive innovation** — launches *worse* on the mainstream metric, but cheaper, simpler, smaller, or more convenient. It takes root at the low end or in a new market the incumbent ignores, then improves fast enough to eventually satisfy mainstream needs — and takes the market from below.

## Why Incumbents Miss It
- **Resource-dependence / customer capture** — resources flow to what current customers and current margins reward. Disruptive products serve customers you don't have yet, at margins you don't want.
- **Asymmetric motivation** — the disruptor's beachhead (low-margin, small) is precisely the segment the incumbent is *happy to cede*. Retreating upmarket feels like a win right up until there's no room left.
- **Markets that don't exist can't be analyzed.** Disruptive markets are unknowable in advance; big-company planning processes demand data that doesn't yet exist, so the opportunity gets screened out.
- **Value networks** — you're embedded in a web of suppliers, customers, and cost structures tuned to the current product. That network can't perceive value in the disruptive one.

## Key Frameworks
- **Technology supply vs market demand.** Technology improves faster than customers' needs rise. Once "good enough" is reached, the basis of competition shifts (functionality → reliability → convenience → price), opening the door to disruptors.
- **RPV — Resources, Processes, Values.** What an organization *can* do is bounded by its Resources (what it has), Processes (how it works), and Values (what it prioritizes — especially the margins it will accept). Processes and Values that make you strong become the rigid constraints that stop you disrupting yourself.
- **The remedy: an autonomous unit.** House a disruptive effort in a separate organization small enough to get excited about small wins, with its own cost structure and freedom to serve a market the mothership disdains.

## Kiln Applications
- **Watch for "good enough."** When a client's category hits over-served buyers, competition shifts to convenience and price — the moment a simpler/cheaper GTM motion can win. Sell into that shift.
- **The Kiln itself is a disruptor.** An AI-native, low-overhead GTM model attacks legacy agencies from a beachhead they're happy to ignore. Don't apologize for starting "low end" — that's the classic entry.
- **Don't only build what current clients ask for.** Their requests point at sustaining work; keep a separate track for the motion the market will want next.
- **Diagnostic line for pitches:** "Your competitor isn't the company that does what you do better — it's the one doing something you consider beneath you."
