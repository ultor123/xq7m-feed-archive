# The Innovator's Solution — Clayton Christensen & Michael Raynor
> Distilled for speaking, pitching, and daily insight. Framework notes in own words.

## Core Thesis
The sequel flips the dilemma: if disruption predictably kills incumbents, how do you *engineer* yourself to be the disruptor? The book turns disruption from an accident into a repeatable set of design choices.

## Two Kinds of Disruption
- **Low-end disruption** — enter where the incumbent has over-served customers with too many features, win with a cheaper "good enough" offer, then move upmarket.
- **New-market disruption** — compete against *nonconsumption*. Serve people who lacked the money or skill to use the old product at all. The bar is low because the alternative is nothing.
The best target is usually **nonconsumption** — no incumbent is defending it, and the customer is thrilled with a modest solution.

## Jobs To Be Done
Customers don't buy products; they **"hire" a product to do a job** that arises in their lives. Segment by the job, not by demographics or product category. Get the job right and the features, channel, and message follow. Get it wrong and you build a better product nobody hires.

## Where Profit Lives (Conservation of Attractive Profits)
- When a product isn't yet good enough, the money is in **integrated, interdependent** architectures — you must control the whole stack to make it work.
- Once it's more than good enough, it **modularizes and commoditizes**; profit migrates to the adjacent stage that is now the bottleneck (often components or a new layer).
- **Law of Conservation of Attractive Profits:** when one thing commoditizes, something adjacent de-commoditizes. Track where "not good enough" is — that's where margin is.

## Building It Right
- **Good money vs bad money.** Be *impatient for profit* (forces a viable model early) but *patient for growth* (don't force premature scale). Capital that demands huge growth immediately pushes you to cram a disruptive product into a mainstream market and kills it.
- **Schools of experience.** Hire managers for the problems they've actually wrestled, not their résumé polish — a disruptive venture needs people who've navigated ambiguity, not just run a mature P&L.
- **Separate organization, again.** Match the opportunity to a unit whose values (acceptable margins, cost structure) fit the disruptive economics.

## Kiln Applications
- **Lead every discovery call with the job.** Not "what do you sell" but "what job is your buyer hiring your product to do, and what are they hiring instead of you?" That reframing is a differentiator most agencies skip.
- **Sell into nonconsumption.** The Kiln's highest-leverage clients may be companies doing *no* structured outbound yet — competing against nothing beats fighting an incumbent agency for a switched account.
- **Read the commoditization line.** As basic outbound tooling commoditizes, margin moves to strategy, data, and orchestration — position The Kiln at the de-commoditizing layer, not the commoditized one.
- **Impatient for profit:** prove each engagement pays before scaling headcount into it.
